RightSizeCPU Bench: combining trees and language models

Big release (big-1) · round 21 rerun · trees × LLM hybrids
Abstract

A task's CPU request is set before it runs. Below the task's real peak, the kernel throttles it and it finishes late; above it, cores sit idle. RightSizeCPU Bench measures how well different approaches predict a task's peak CPU, the busiest 15-second average, from its source code, its input files' metadata and its earlier runs. Each approach is scored on the share of runs whose predicted peak lands within ±25% of the measured one. In round 20, on 9,900 measured runs, gradient-boosted trees did well on code like their training data and poorly on two held-out families, while fine-tuned language models dropped much less. This round reruns the same corpus and adds two ways to combine them. A stacked hybrid writes the trees' prediction into the language model's prompt and trains the model with it; the training hints come from trees fitted without the run's task. A pooled hybrid multiplies the two models' probabilities for each core count, with a weight on the trees tuned on the validation split.

On 1,634 test runs of new code from the training families, the hybrids are slightly the best: the stacked 1.7B decision model scores 98.5%, against 97.7% for the same model alone and 94.6% for the trees. That gain is about the size of the noise. On 1,800 runs from the two unseen families, every hybrid scores 4 to 17 points below the language model it is built from, and most land near the trees: the stacked 1.7B scores 71.7%, against 85.6% alone and 68.4% for the trees. The hybrids follow the trees, gaining where the trees are right and losing where they are wrong. The likely reason is that both learn how far to trust the trees on data where the trees are right: the training hints and the validation split both come from the training families.

Four findings

  1. 98.5%On familiar code, the hybrids add under a point. The stacked 1.7B decision model has the best test score (98.5% against 97.7% alone). The pooled 4B has the lowest error (0.21 cores) and cost (−5.6% CPU regret). With 1,634 test runs, a share near 98% has a 95% interval of about ±0.7 points.
  2. −4 to −17On new code, every hybrid is worse than its language model alone. The points lost on the unseen families range from 4.1 (pooled 4B) to 16.6 (the 1.7B pooled after stacking). Re-fitting an unchanged arm moved its unseen score by at most 7.1 points between rounds, so these drops are not run-to-run noise.
  3. 48.0%The hybrids follow the trees. On pyarrow's CSV reader, the trees score 48.0% and the decision models 89%; the hybrids land at 50–77%. On DuckDB SQL, where the trees are right (88.8%), most hybrids gain 3.5 to 8.5 points over their language model.
  4. 95%Trust in the trees is learned where they are right. The stacked models' out-of-fold hints are right about 95% of the time, so copying them is what training rewards. The pool weight is tuned on validation, where the trees miss 5.0% and the language model 2.0%, so it settles at 0.5–0.7. On the unseen families the trees miss 31.6%. The pool with the lowest weight, 0.5, lost least.
Setup

How the benchmark works

Workloads
11 families of generated Flyte task code: NumPy BLAS, polars, DuckDB SQL, scikit-learn ensembles, pandas, Python thread pools, process pools, PyTorch on CPU, pyarrow CSV, I/O-bound fetch loops, and a serial phase followed by a parallel one. 450 tasks per family, each run twice on inputs of different sizes, for 30 to 60 seconds. The corpus is round 20's, reused unchanged.
Labels
Each run gets 7 pinned cores and samples its CPU time every second. The label is the smallest of 16 core counts (0.25 to 8) that covers the busiest sliding 15-second window.
Splits
Split by task: 5,628 train, 838 validation and 1,634 test runs from 9 families, plus 1,800 runs from 2 families held out entirely (DuckDB SQL and pyarrow CSV). Settings are tuned on validation; test and unseen are scored once.
Single approaches
Re-fit from round 20. Rules: one static request; the largest past peak times a margin; the largest pool size the code declares. Trees: gradient-boosted quantile regression on 71 numeric features, read as a distribution over cells (gbt-dist). Language models: a zero-shot 27B; Qwen3-1.7B fine-tuned with LoRA to write its answer (SFT); and decision models on Qwen3-1.7B and 4B, whose head scores each core count written in the prompt.
Stacked hybrids
The trees' three most likely cells, their pick and their mean CPU are written into the prompt just before the options, and SFT or a decision model is trained with them. Training runs get hints from trees fitted on the other 4 of 5 folds of tasks; validation, test and unseen runs get hints from trees fitted on all of train.
Pooled hybrids
A logarithmic opinion pool: p(cell) ∝ ptreesw · pLLM1−w. w is picked from 0, 0.1 … 1 on validation. Every language model that outputs a distribution is pooled, including the stacked ones. Nothing is retrained.

Key terms

Peak CPU
The highest average core usage in any 15-second window of the run: the busiest value Union's monitoring can show.
Within ±25%
The predicted peak is within 25% of the measured peak, or within 0.25 core for small peaks. The headline metric.
Out-of-fold
A prediction for a training run from a model fitted without that run's task: what the trees would say about code they have not seen.
Trees weight w
How much the pool listens to the trees: 0 is the language model alone, 1 is the trees alone.
Unseen families
The two families no approach trained on. They test whether an approach generalizes to new code and new pool-size APIs.
